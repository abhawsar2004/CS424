# Task 3: Data description and domain questions

Our dataset is made up of study and work sessions that participants reported through our Google Form after finishing them, between September 21 and September 29, 2026. Each row is one session. It records the start and end time, task type, planned length, minutes until the first distraction, number of distractions, a focus rating from 1 to 10, location, noise level, people present, sleep, phone screen time, caffeine, focus strategies, and an overall effectiveness rating from 1 to 5. We added the effectiveness rating after the pilot so participants could judge the session as a whole, separately from how focused they felt. Looking at the responses, most sessions were studying, took place at home, and were described as quiet. Focus ratings leaned high, and nobody rated a session below 4. Effectiveness ratings were more spread out, from 2 to 5, and generally rose with focus, though not always. One session was rated extremely effective despite a middling focus rating and ten distractions. Sessions happened at many times of day, from early morning to late at night. Coverage is uneven, though. Apart from home and the library, most places appear only once. Participants only ever chose Very Quiet, Quiet, or Moderate for noise, so we have no noisy sessions to compare against. The sample is also small, the people who chose to fill out the form may differ from those who did not, and everyone estimated their own focus and distractions. Several answers were hard to use as written. Sleep and planned time mix hours and minutes, one date has the wrong year, two sessions have AM/PM mix-ups, one distraction count was entered as "20+", and a few sleep and screen-time answers are blank. A zero for time to first distraction means no distraction occurred, which the revised form now states explicitly, so we treat it separately rather than as a distraction at the very start.

Turning each completed session into one row let us record when and where someone worked and compare their reported focus with their surroundings and habits. The row leaves out what caused each distraction, how long it lasted, and how focus changed over the course of the session. Noise is a person's description rather than a sound measurement. We chose simple categories and numbers so sessions would be easier to compare, but those choices make a complicated experience look simpler than it really was. Because of this, we look for possible patterns without claiming that any one factor caused better focus.

After seeing the responses, we want to ask four questions. We picked them because the data has these attributes and some variation in them, not because we already know the answers.

1. **Do quieter sessions tend to have fewer distractions and higher focus ratings?** This connects noise level with distraction count and focus rating and shows whether the surroundings might matter, within the three noise levels we actually observed.
2. **Does the time before the first distraction differ between task types?** This uses task type and minutes to first distraction, since studying, reading, writing, and meetings may demand attention in different ways. We set aside sessions with no distractions when comparing that time.
3. **How do focus ratings and distraction counts differ by location?** This uses location, focus rating, and distraction count. Because most places appear only once, we compare home, the library, and all other places grouped together.
4. **Is there a pattern between sleep the previous night and focus rating?** This uses sleep and focus rating, after converting all sleep answers to hours and leaving out blank answers.

Our first plan listed more factors to study, including phone use, interaction with other people, caffeine, and focus strategies. The responses changed our focus. Most participants reported no caffeine and no focus strategy, so there was too little variation to compare those groups meaningfully. Phone screen time had the same mixed-unit problems as sleep, and some answers were missing. Noise, task type, location, and sleep had clearer variation, so our questions moved toward them. The pilot also showed that some answers used different units or labels, so our questions now account for missing or unclear values.
