# Task 3: Data description and domain questions

Our dataset currently has 18 study or work sessions, each with 20 attributes. Each row is one session that a participant reported through our Google Form after finishing it. The sessions are dated September 21 through September 28, 2026, although half of them fall on September 22 and 23. Each row records the session's start and end time, task type, planned length, minutes until the first distraction, number of distractions, focus rating from 1 to 10, location, noise level, people present, sleep, phone screen time, caffeine, and focus strategies. The tasks include studying (7), meetings (4), reading, writing, and homework, and the sessions ran anywhere from 1 AM to 9 PM. Coverage is uneven, though. Ten sessions happened at home and three at the library, while the bus, campus, office, lounge, and CDRLC appear only once each. Participants only ever chose Very Quiet, Quiet, or Moderate for noise, so we have no noisy sessions to compare against. Eighteen sessions is still a small sample, the people who chose to fill out the form may differ from those who did not, and everyone estimated their own focus and distractions. Several answers were hard to use as written. Sleep and planned time mix hours and minutes, one date has the wrong year, two sessions have AM/PM mix-ups, one distraction count was entered as "20+", one sleep answer is blank, and the overall-effectiveness question we added after the pilot was left blank in every response. A zero for time to first distraction means no distraction occurred, which the revised form now states explicitly, so we treat it separately rather than as a distraction at the very start.

Turning each completed session into one row let us record when and where someone worked and compare their reported focus with their surroundings and habits. The row leaves out what caused each distraction, how long it lasted, and how focus changed over the course of the session. Noise is a person's description rather than a sound measurement. We chose simple categories and numbers so sessions would be easier to compare, but those choices make a complicated experience look simpler than it really was. Because of this, we look for possible patterns without claiming that any one factor caused better focus.

After seeing the responses, we want to ask four questions. First, do quieter sessions tend to have fewer distractions and higher focus ratings? This connects noise level with distraction count and focus rating and shows whether the surroundings might matter, within the three noise levels we actually observed. Second, does the time before the first distraction differ between task types? This uses task type and minutes to first distraction, since studying, reading, writing, and meetings may demand attention in different ways. We set aside sessions with no distractions when comparing that time. Third, how do focus ratings and distraction counts differ by location? This uses location, focus rating, and distraction count. Because most places appear only once, we compare home, the library, and all other places grouped together. Fourth, is there a pattern between sleep the previous night and focus rating? This uses sleep and focus rating, after converting all sleep answers to hours and leaving out the blank answer. We picked these questions because the data has these attributes and some variation in them, not because we already know the answers.

Our first plan listed more factors to study, including phone use, interaction with other people, caffeine, and focus strategies. The responses changed our focus. Ten of the 18 participants reported no caffeine and ten reported no focus strategy, so there was too little variation to compare those groups meaningfully. Phone screen time had the same mixed-unit problems as sleep, and two answers were missing. Noise, task type, location, and sleep had clearer variation, so our questions moved toward them. The pilot also showed that some answers used different units or labels, so our questions now account for missing or unclear values.
