# Task 3: Data description and domain questions

Our dataset currently has 18 study or work sessions shown in our collection sheet. Each row is one session that a participant reported after finishing it through our Google Form. The rows shown are dated September 21 through September 28, 2026, and include the session time, type of task, planned length, time until the first distraction, number of distractions, focus rating from 1 to 10, location, noise level, sleep, and caffeine. We collected different kinds of tasks, including studying, reading, writing, homework, and meetings. Sessions took place at home, in the library, on a bus, and in a few other places. They also happened at different times of day and had different noise levels and focus ratings. This gives us some differences to explore, but 18 sessions are still a small sample. The people who chose to fill out the form may also differ from people who did not, and everyone estimated their own focus and distractions. Some answers are hard to use as written: sleep and planned time mix hours and minutes, one date has the wrong year, one sleep answer is blank, and a few time ranges are unclear. A zero for time to first distraction seems to mean there was no distraction, so we should check the original answer before treating it as a distraction at the start.

Making one completed session into one row let us record when and where someone worked and compare their reported focus with their surroundings and habits. At the same time, the row leaves out what caused each distraction, how long it lasted, and how the person felt during different parts of the session. Noise is also a person's description rather than a sound measurement. We chose simple categories and numbers so sessions would be easier to compare, but those choices make a complicated experience look simpler than it really was. We should look for possible patterns without saying that one factor caused someone to focus better.

After seeing the responses, we want to ask four questions. First, do quieter sessions tend to have fewer distractions and higher focus ratings? This connects noise level with distraction count and focus rating and helps us see whether the surroundings might matter. Second, does the time before the first distraction differ between task types? This uses task type and minutes to first distraction, since studying, reading, writing, and meetings may demand attention in different ways. We would set aside sessions with no distractions when comparing that time. Third, how do focus ratings and distraction counts differ by location? This uses location, focus rating, and distraction count; home and the library occur several times, but we need to be careful with locations represented by just one session. Fourth, is there a pattern between sleep the previous night and focus rating? This uses sleep and focus rating, after putting sleep answers into the same unit and leaving the blank answer out of that comparison. We picked these questions because the sheet has those attributes and some variation in them, not because we already know what the answers will be.

Our first collection plan listed many possible things to study, including phone use, interaction with other people, caffeine, and focus strategies. Looking at the collected responses shifted our attention toward the questions above because the visible sheet gives us clearer ways to compare noise, task type, location, sleep, distractions, and focus. The pilot also showed that some answers used different units or labels, so our questions now account for missing or unclear values. Our Task 1 document describes the planned attributes but does not include the original four questions. We need to add those original questions to the final report to explain any changes to their exact wording accurately.
