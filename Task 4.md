# Task 4: Task abstractions

For our first question about noise and focus, the main action is to compare. The targets are the distributions of distraction counts and focus ratings within each noise level. We chose this because we want to see whether sessions described as quieter generally have fewer distractions or higher focus ratings. Comparing whole distributions rather than single averages matters here, because we also need to see how many sessions fall in each group. A pattern based on one or two sessions would be weak.

For our second question about task type, the action is again to compare, and the target is the distribution of time to first distraction for each task type. This helps us see whether some tasks tend to be interrupted sooner and whether sessions of the same type behave similarly. There is also a second, smaller task: identifying sessions with no distraction. These are outliers in meaning rather than in value, because their recorded zero does not mean the first distraction happened at the start, so they must be separated before comparing.

For our third question about location, the primary action is to compare the distributions of focus ratings and distraction counts across places. We also have a secondary task, which is to look up how many sessions each place has so we can identify places that are represented too thinly to trust. We want to know whether focus and interruptions seem different at home, in the library, or elsewhere. One response from a bus or an office should not be treated as a reliable picture of that place.

For our fourth question about sleep, the action is to identify the correlation between sleep the night before and focus during the session. We also want to identify outliers, meaning sessions where someone slept very little but focused well, or the reverse. The targets are reported sleep hours and focus ratings for individual sessions, since the relationship only shows up when both values are seen together for each session. Before doing that, we need to convert the sleep answers to one unit and leave out missing answers.

Writing these tasks changed how we think about our questions. At first, we were asking broad questions about what might affect focus. Framing them as actions and targets showed that three of our questions are really comparisons of distributions across categories, while only the sleep question is about a relationship between two numbers. That difference will shape our sketches. It also made clear that checking group sizes, missing answers, and the meaning of zero are tasks in their own right rather than side notes. These abstractions describe what a person needs to learn from the data. We will decide how to draw them when we make our sketches.
