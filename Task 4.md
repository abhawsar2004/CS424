# Task 4: Task abstractions

For our first question about noise and focus, the main action is to compare groups of sessions. The targets are the distraction counts and focus ratings for each noise level. We chose this because we want to see whether sessions described as quieter generally have fewer distractions or higher focus ratings. We also need to notice how many sessions are in each group, since a pattern based on one or two sessions would be weak.

For our second question about task type, the action is to compare when the first distraction happened across different kinds of work. The target is the time to first distraction for sessions grouped by task type. This helps us see whether some tasks tend to be interrupted sooner and whether sessions of the same type are similar. A session with no distractions should be identified separately, because its recorded zero does not mean the first distraction happened at the start.

For our third question about location, we want to compare the focus ratings and distraction counts across places and identify places with very few sessions. These are our targets because we want to know whether focus and interruptions seem different at home, in the library, or elsewhere. Counting the sessions at each place matters: we should not treat one response from a bus or office as a reliable picture of that place.

For our fourth question about sleep, the action is to explore the relationship between sleep the night before and focus during the session. The targets are reported sleep hours and focus ratings for individual sessions. We want to see whether people who reported more sleep also tended to report better focus, while noticing exceptions. Before doing that, we need to put the sleep answers in the same unit and leave missing answers out of this comparison.

Writing these tasks changed how we think about our questions. At first, we were asking broad questions about what might affect focus. Now we can say exactly what someone should compare or look for in the data. We also noticed that checking group sizes, missing answers, and the meaning of zero is part of answering the questions fairly. These tasks describe what a person needs to learn from the data; we will decide how to draw them when we make our sketches.
